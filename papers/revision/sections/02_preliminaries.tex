\section{Preliminaries and convention}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

Let $\mathcal H=\C^{n\times d}$ and let $L:\C^n\to\C^n$ be a finite
self-adjoint graph operator with spectrum in $[0,2]$.  For the normalized
Laplacian used in our experiments, $L=U\Lambda U^*$ and the graph Fourier
coefficients of $h\in\mathcal H$ are $\widehat h=U^*h$.  A scalar function
$g$ on the spectrum defines $g(L)=Ug(\Lambda)U^*$.  Signal norms are
Frobenius norms $\norm{\cdot}_F$; tuple-valued coefficient maps use the
corresponding direct-sum norm $\norm{\cdot}_{\oplus,F}$; and
$\norm{\cdot}_{\op}$ is the induced spectral operator norm.  No result
below requires a simple spectrum: repeated eigenvalues receive the same
scalar multiplier under functional calculus.

Write
\begin{equation}
  \D=\{z\in\C:|z|<1\}
  \label{eq:open-unit-disk}
\end{equation}
for the open unit disk.

The self-adjointness assumption is part of the scientific contract.  The
canonical implementation rejects nonfinite, negative-weight, or asymmetric
graph inputs at the operator boundary and issues a hash-bearing validated
Laplacian token.  Directed input can instead pass through a separate
reciprocal-mean preprocessor, $A\leftarrow(A+A^*)/2$, which records its policy
and input/output hashes.  Symmetrization is never an implicit model operation.

We use the Cayley map and elementary Blaschke factor
\begin{equation}
  \cayley(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda-i}{\lambda+i},
  \qquad
  B_\alpha(z)=\frac{z-\alpha}{1-\overline\alpha z},
  \qquad |\alpha|<1.
  \label{eq:definitions}
\end{equation}
For a root multiset $\mathcal R=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_R)$, write
$B_{\mathcal R}=\prod_{r=1}^R B_{\alpha_r}$ and
\begin{equation}
  \Tcal_{\mathcal R}=B_{\mathcal R}(\cayley(L)).
  \label{eq:forward-convention}
\end{equation}
The inverse convention is the conjugate multiplier and is never used
implicitly.  In every linearity, adjoint, conditioning, and Jacobian result,
the roots are fixed independently of the signal being analyzed.

Our canonical admissible-root parameterization is radial:
\begin{equation}
  \alpha=r_{\max}\sigma(s)e^{i\vartheta},
  \qquad 0<r_{\max}<1.
  \label{eq:radial-root}
\end{equation}
It enforces $|\alpha|<r_{\max}$; independent Cartesian clipping does not.
The optional center--width parameterization
\begin{equation}
  \alpha=\cayley(\mu+i\gamma),
  \qquad \mu\in[0,2],
  \qquad 0<\gamma_{\min}\leq\gamma\leq\gamma_{\max}<\infty,
  \label{eq:center-width-root}
\end{equation}
places the mapped zero exactly at $\mu+i\gamma$ and its conjugate target pole
at $\mu-i\gamma$.  Its pole margin from the graph spectral interval is
$\operatorname{dist}(\mu-i\gamma,[0,2])=\gamma$; the implementation emits
the mapped zero, mapped pole, and this margin as diagnostics.  Any run using
this optional family must freeze and record $\gamma_{\min}$ and
$\gamma_{\max}$.  The radial parameterization is less restrictive: its mapped
real-axis center may lie
outside $[0,2]$, in which case the mapped location and the response maximum
over the observed interval must be reported separately.  A radial angular
anchor such as $\cayley(\mu)$ is not called an exact spectral center.

The sparse realization approximates a target multiplier $g$ by
\begin{equation}
  p_K(L)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}c_k T_k(L-I),
  \label{eq:cheb-realization}
\end{equation}
where $T_k$ is the degree-$k$ Chebyshev polynomial.  We write
$T_\ell=g_\ell(L)$ for an exact Blaschke--Cayley factor and
$\widetilde T_{\ell,K}=p_{\ell,K}(L)$ for its finite realization.  The exact
target is rational and has mapped poles; $p_{\ell,K}$ is a polynomial and
has no literal finite poles.  Thus, for the finite implementation, ``mapped
pole'' always means a pole of the parameterized target response, not of the
implemented polynomial.
